# YACHATDAC

## Living Work — Caring for Country
Field notes format. Three layers: the work, infrastructure and technology, and what the work produces.




Field notes from Turraburra
YACHATDAC manages Turraburra, 8,870 hectares of Iningai Country 120km
north of Barcaldine in Central Western Queensland. The property was bought
back for the Iningai people in 2019.
Iningai Rangers deliver the work: fire-stick farming, land restoration and management, wildlife
conservation and site protection, on a property that runs on its own water, power and
communications.




Our challenges
[ Expandable list. Each opens to context and what is being done. ]

A century of overgrazing
Turraburra was run as a sheep and cattle station from the 1880s. Continuous grazing strips
groundcover, compacts soil and stops native grasses setting seed. Fencing lets us rest
paddocks; seed collection and replanting rebuild what grazing removed.

Degraded springs and waterholes
Springs are the most valuable water here and the first thing stock destroy. Restoration is slow:
clear the hole, plant around it, keep water up to it through the first dry.

Soil carbon loss and erosion
Testing here found carbon beginning only 30 centimetres down. Groundcover, right-way fire and
rested paddocks build it back. Worth measuring before you start work rather than after.

Wrong-way fire and wildfire risk
Without regular cool burning, fuel builds and the fire that comes is hot enough to kill trees,
sterilise soil and damage sites. Cool burns break Country into a patchwork.

Weeds and feral animals
Ferals foul waterholes, eat regrowth and damage rock shelters. Weeds move in wherever ground
is disturbed. Control is continuous and targeted.

Extreme heat, drought and flood
The highest-likelihood risk on this property. Workplans are built loose enough to move around the
season.
Distance from town
Barcaldine is 120km south, Aramac the last town. Everything carries freight cost and waiting
time. Machinery is serviced and rebuilt on site.

No reliable phone reception
No dependable mobile coverage apart from patches on the escarpment. A safety issue before it
is an inconvenience. See Infrastructure.

Water security across 8,870 hectares
Stock, camp, nursery and firefighting water all come off one system. A standing priority rather
than a project with an end date.

Damage and access to cultural sites
Marra Wonga carries graffiti going back over a century, and artefacts have been removed since
the late 1800s. Roads go around sites; access is guided.

Species we have not yet identified
You cannot protect or claim credit for what you have not recorded. Acoustic recorders and
cameras are building that record.

Ranger funding ahead of income
Grant cycles are shorter than the work. Losing ranger funding before commercial income is
established is the single biggest risk to everything here.

Natural capital market volatility
Prices move and methodologies change. The approach is several natural capital products rather
than one.




Iningai Rangers
Rangers run the cool burns, the fencing, the seed collection, the spring and wetland restoration,
the weed and feral control, the machinery and the site protection. Ranger workplans set the
year’s work across the property. Training partnerships are being built so the jobs here can be
filled from community.

The work
What Rangers do on the ground. Alternating photograph and text.
01

Fire-stick farming
     Low-intensity cool burning at the right time of year — reducing fuel loads,
     encouraging germination and breaking Country into a patchwork that carries
     more life than unburnt or hot-burnt ground.
Rangers burn right way and look for black ash rather than scorched ground. Burns run as
workshops as well as work. Soil is tested before and after: carbon here began only 30
centimetres down, and one paddock was recorded working as a carbon sink within eight to nine
months of a cool burn.


02

Springs and waterholes
     Bringing cultural wetlands and spring networks back to holding water on their
     own.
One spring had been trampled flat by a century of stock. Rangers cleaned the waterhole out,
planted grasses around it and carted a thousand litres to it twice a day through drought. Eight
days in it held a puddle. The next morning there was a koala standing at it.


03

Seed and groundcover
     Collecting seed when Country produces it, holding it, and putting it back into
     revegetation.
Seed is available when the season decides, not when a work plan says so, which is why the year
is organised around the country rather than the other way round.


04

Fencing and paddocks
     New fencing built corner assembly by corner assembly, giving paddocks that
     can be managed separately.
Rangers built it themselves, learning the job as it went. Without separate paddocks the flux tower
comparison would not have been possible.


05

Weeds and feral animals
     Continuous control targeted at the places doing the most damage —
     waterholes, regrowth and rock shelters.
Control is ongoing rather than a one-off campaign, and runs alongside the restoration work.


06

Sites and access
     Protecting Marra Wonga and the other recorded sites, and controlling how
     people reach them.
Road alignments were set from cultural mapping so routes work around sites rather than through
them. Ecological and cultural mapping tells us what is where and what must not be disturbed.


07

Monitoring
     Recording what is here and what changes, so management decisions and credit
     claims both rest on evidence.
Four acoustic recorders log birds and frogs into a database held with QUT, two of them at the
springs. Cameras on the towers photograph five hundred metres around. Soil samples from the
bore are held by QUT.




Infrastructure and technology
What it takes to run a property 120 kilometres from the nearest town.
[ Dark section. Schematic diagram — Starlink to router to mesh; UHF coverage radius; bore to tanks, camp and stock;
solar feeding the monitoring gear. No photographs. ]

COMMUNICATIONS  Starlink uplink bridged to an external router · wifi mesh with three extenders plus one
in the campground · UHF radio in the house, the shed and nearly every vehicle · about ten UHF
handhelds, roughly 5km range from the house
WATER  480-metre bore at Lancewood Ridge · tanks, pumps and lines across the property · filters on a
six-month replacement cycle · stock, camp, nursery and firefighting water off one system
POWER   Solar with wifi-enabled regulators · data can be pulled off the regulators remotely · 240 volt
supply to the mesh network
MONITORING GEAR   Two flux towers — managed Country and grazed control · four acoustic recorders,
two at the springs · cameras on the towers, 500m radius · soil samples every six metres from the
bore, held with QUT
MACHINERY  Grader, buggies, vehicles and heavy plant · serviced, repaired and rebuilt on site · register
down to serial and filter numbers · service history with hours and kilometres, updated every visit
COMING    Secure Ranger Base · research facilities and nurseries · walking tracks and camping facilities
· staff housing
